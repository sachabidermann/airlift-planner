Method

1. Airfield survivability

The USGS ShakeMap gives estimated shaking intensity (MMI) on a grid around every significant quake, real or simulated. The intensity at each airport is turned into a probability that it can accept relief flights in the first days. The curve is a judgement anchored on nine real events, not a fitted model, and every backtest prints what the model saw at the airports that mattered so the curve can be argued with. The grid is checked against USGS's own city intensities (see the verification table in the repository).

2. Demand

USGS PAGER counts people exposed at each intensity. For US scenarios without PAGER, exposure is rebuilt from Census county and city populations sampled against the shaking grid. People at MMI VIII and above are the airbridge priority: severe damage, roads often cut. Each needs a daily minimum of food, medicine and a share of a family shelter kit spread over the first week. Water is excluded, because at 15 litres per person per day it is never flown at scale.

3. Airbridge

Heavy jets fly into a gateway; C-130s shuttle the last leg into strips inside the damage zone. Capacity is ramp spots times daily turnarounds times payload, discounted by usability. Gateways are chosen in the affected country first; cargo landing near the damage centre counts as delivered by road, fading to nothing at the forward radius. Regional airports are capped at C-17 class aircraft.

delivered = min(inflow, road share + shuttle tonnes)

Limitations

  • Not an operational tool. The usability curve is judgement. Aircraft figures are public planning approximations.
  • Straight-line distances. No fuel stocks, customs, handling equipment, weather, airspace or road network, which is why the model diverges from reality in Turkey 2023 and Myanmar 2025.
  • The airport table is today's, so replays of older events can offer airports that did not exist at the time.
  • Small quakes without a ShakeMap fall back to a published intensity-vs-distance formula (Allen, Wald and Worden, 2012).
